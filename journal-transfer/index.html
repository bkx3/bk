Day One to Apple Journal.

A Day One JSON export became a repeatable Apple Journal import workflow using Python, a folder-based handoff format, and an Apple Shortcut.

I've been journaling every day for nearly 5,000 days. This habit was inspired by re-discovering a journal I kept in 2001 of a school trip to Belize. I was able to re-live every day of that trip by reading my messy, lazy 15 year-old handwriting. If an old paper journal could teleport me back to the jungle in 2001, I assume that a modern digital journal with photos, videos and audio recordings will probably do wonders for me as an old man.

I wrote my first modern, daily entry on November 8, 2013, and haven't stopped since. I've also gone back in time and used photos to jog my memories and tell stories from the early 1990s and forward. This journal is a very thorough archive of the past ~13 years of my life, and has a lot of the highlights leading up to that time.

These entries have been kept in the Day One app, which was acquired by Automattic. This archive is incredibly important to me, and relying on a single private company to keep it safe and portable became a point of concern. I recently used AI assistance to help convert the somewhat messy output of a Day One archive into entries in Apple Journal, which allows for export and archiving into clean HTML pages.

It's still to be decided if I keep writing entries in Day One or Apple Journal, but it's a relief to know that my entries now exist in multiple places, and can be easily archived for ingest from future platforms. By utilizing the following workflow, or pointing your AI assistant at it, you can do the same.

How it worked

  1. Export Day One as JSON, including the media attachment folders.
  2. Use a Python script to read each entry, preserve the original date and time zone, clean up Markdown, and match media back to the right journal entry.
  3. Generate a Shortcut-friendly folder for each entry, with small control files for the body, heading, date, timestamp, and media.
  4. Group those entry folders into batches of 500 so the import can be resumed or retried without starting over.
  5. Run an Apple Shortcut that loops through one batch at a time, reads the files, attaches any media, and calls Apple Journal's Create Journal Entry action.
  6. Export Apple Journal afterward and compare it against the generated source data to find missing entries, duplicates, and near-duplicates.

What was in each folder

  • Body.txt held the journal entry text.
  • Heading.txt held the entry title.
  • Date.txt, ISODate.txt, and UnixTimestamp.txt preserved the original date in formats Shortcuts could use.
  • Media/ contained copied photos for that entry.
  • NeedsAI.txt and AIPrompt.txt were helper files for entries that needed title cleanup.

Scripts and Prompts

  • dayone_to_shortcuts_manifest.py generated the folder-based import feed used by the Shortcut.
  • reconcile_journal_export.py compared the generated feed against a later Apple Journal HTML export.
  • Agent guide gives a more complete setup and execution path for adapting the workflow.

Troubleshooting

  • Apple Journal does not have a normal bulk import tool, so the workflow had to go through Shortcuts.
  • One giant Shortcut run was unreliable, which is why the import was split into batches.
  • Day One stores image references in the entry text; those had to be removed from the body while keeping the real media attachments.
  • Some entries had long first lines, no clear title, or Markdown formatting, so titles needed a cleanup pass.
  • Date handling mattered because the entries needed to keep their original journal dates, not the date they were imported.
  • Media had to be copied into predictable locations so Shortcuts could attach it to the right entry.
  • The final reconciliation step exposed missing entries, duplicates, and near-duplicates that needed smaller recovery passes.
